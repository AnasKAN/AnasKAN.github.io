The student scrambles, the teacher gets dragged

Each batch the student takes one noisy step downhill, then the teacher moves a fraction (1 − m) of the way toward it. As the learning rate decays and m rises, the student calms down and settles onto the teacher. The terrain is invented: it stands in for "how good these weights are," not for DINO's actual loss.

What moves each batch

The student steps by −lr · (slope + noise + pull toward teacher). The noise stands in for the minibatch: each batch sees different images and crops, so its direction is a little off. The pull stands in for DINO's actual objective, which is to match the teacher's output. Then the teacher does θt ← m·θt + (1−m)·θs, exactly as in DINO.

Why it settles at the end

DINO decays the learning rate along a cosine curve after a short warm-up, and raises m toward 1. Smaller steps make the student's noise shrink, so it stops wandering. Its pull toward the teacher then dominates, and the teacher absorbs its last small moves. By the final epoch the two sit almost on top of each other.

Why the teacher ends up better

The teacher is a running average of everywhere the student has been. Random zig-zags cancel out in that average, while a direction the student keeps returning to builds up. In the paper this shows up as the teacher's k-NN accuracy staying above the student's throughout training.

What is invented here

The terrain, the 2D space, the noise and the score are all made up. Real DINO has millions of weights and a single loss per batch: the cross-entropy between the teacher's output and the student's output. The teacher gets no loss and no gradients. Real runs also don't always land in the deepest basin; try "New random run" to see a few end up elsewhere.
